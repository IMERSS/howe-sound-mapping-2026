In Memory of Plants

The Sḵwx̱wú7mesh Úxwumixw (Squamish Nation) have cared for the lands and waters of Átl’ḵa7tsem since time immemorial.

The memory preserved here is much more limited: collected specimens, names written down on lists, and observations shared through iNaturalist, reaching back to 1897.

Each record is a moment when someone stopped to take note of a plant.

Some plants have long been recorded, while others have appeared only in more recent years. Still others have gone unseen for decades, quietly disappearing from the record.

The lost plants of Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound

This gallery shows native species once reported for the Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound Biosphere Region, which have not been seen in recent decades. Some may be lost to us—overlooked, forgotten, or waiting to be rediscovered. Others may be lost because of us, through impacts like habitat change or the introduction of invasive species.

Pick a plant from the gallery to see where and when it was last found, then join local naturalists and community members in searching for it, sharing what you find with the Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound Biosphere Region project on iNaturalist.

Howe Sound’s vascular plant diversity

Over one thousand vascular plant species are known to occur within the Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound Biosphere, represented by 66611 occurrence records. This diverse flora spans nine biogeoclimatic units, from lowland coastal western hemlock forests and associated woodland communities to high elevation subalpine and alpine plant communities.

History of collection activities within the region

While Indigenous knowledge of Átl’ḵa7tsem’s plant life traces back to time immemorial, Western scientific documentation of the region’s flora dates to 1897, with the early collection efforts of James Anderson and Albert J. Hill. Over the last century and a half, early efforts to document the regional flora were most concentrated in the 1920s, and from the 1960s through the 1980s, before expanding significantly in the 21st century with the advent of iNaturalist, with many thousands of observations made by community members through the Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound Biodiversity project.

Community contributions to our knowledge of the regional plant diversity

Through the combined efforts of 3464 individuals, the Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound naturalist community has now documented a total of 1179 vascular plant species. Since 2000, 83 percent of the historically reported plant diversity has been confirmed and 486 new species reports added to the list.

Community members have now documented 84 percent of the total plant diversity known to the region on iNaturalist.

Finding the lost plants

Every plant in this list is a search waiting to happen. This tool suggests where to begin from the pattern of past sightings alone, applying one component of our protocol for detecting local extinction (Simon et al., 2025).

Extirpation probability (EP) runs from 0 (likely still present) to 1 (likely lost locally). Using Solow’s (1993) method, it compares when a species was sighted with the whole period of record, from the region’s first collections in 1897 to 2024. EP is highest for plants that were recorded repeatedly and then stopped being seen. Plants recorded only once or twice tend to score nearer the middle, since so few records say little either way.

Five steps to a target species:

  1. Choose a plant. The table lists the highest EP first. Tick a species, and use Filters to narrow the list, for example to native species.
  2. See where it grew. Each circle on the map is a group of sightings treated as one population, coloured by its EP. Hover over one for its Cluster EP. A smaller Population radius can reveal sites where a plant has gone unseen even though it persists elsewhere. A ⚠ marks plants with records that have no coordinates and so cannot be mapped.
  3. Read its history. Open Records to see who found it, when and where. The dates show what time of year it was collected.
  4. Go looking. Search the places and habitats where it was last recorded, at the season it was last seen.
  5. Share what you find. Post your photos to iNaturalist through the Átl’ḵa7tsem/Howe Sound Biosphere Region project. Whatever you find, the search itself adds to what we know.

A high EP is a reason to go looking, not proof of loss. It does not account for where or how hard people have searched, or for suitable habitat, which the full protocol goes on to weigh.
